# How to use this book

This book takes you from your first note to an intermediate level on piano and guitar. On piano, the aim is to read sheet music well and sight-read new pieces, up to classical pieces at about RCM Level 5 or 6 (the Royal Conservatory levels used across Canada). On guitar, the aim is chords all over the neck, strumming and fingerpicking from chord charts and tab, and easy classical studies. Theory, ear training and improvising run through the whole book, with examples from classical music and from the chord moves behind film, game and anime music.

No music knowledge is assumed. Every term is explained the first time it appears, and the glossary<!-- link: ../reference/glossary.md --> collects them all.

## The stages

## The piano path and the guitar path

Every lesson has a tag under its title: Piano, Guitar, Theory, Reading, Ear, Improvising or Practice. If you want to focus on one instrument for a while, follow its path:

- The piano path is every lesson except the ones tagged Guitar.
- The guitar path is every lesson except the ones tagged Piano.

The [progress page](../progress.md) shows the tag next to every lesson. You can come back for the other instrument's lessons later; nothing on one path depends on the other.

## How a lesson works

A lesson takes 10 to 15 minutes to read. Then comes the part that matters: a 30-minute practice routine that you repeat on several days.

1. Read the lesson with your instrument next to you, and try each example as you go.
2. Do the "Try it" exercises. The answers are in boxes that stay closed until you click them.
3. Practise the routine. It splits the 30 minutes into a warm-up, the new skill, some sight reading or ear training, and a few minutes on the other instrument, so both keep moving.
4. Tick the "Move on when" boxes once you can do each one. Each box is a test, such as "three times in a row with no stops at 60 bpm". Most lessons take three to five days of practice before the boxes are ticked.

At that pace the whole book takes one and a half to two years of daily practice. That is a normal amount of time to get from nothing to an intermediate level, and the lessons are built so that each day's 30 minutes counts.

## Music on the page

Music examples are drawn as sheet music, with guitar tab underneath where it helps. Under each one is a play bar:

- Play and pause.
- Loop, to repeat the example while you play along.
- A tempo control, to slow the example down. Start slow and speed up as it gets easier.
- The notes light up as they play, so you can follow along on the staff.

The sounds are a piano and a steel-string guitar. Audio starts when you press play, so a phone on silent mode may need its ringer switched on.

If you read the book in VS Code instead of on the website, music examples show as text in a notation called ABC. The website is the better way to read the music lessons.

## Practice tools, flashcards and Anki

The [Practice tools](../practice/index.md) tab has drills that run in your browser: note flashcards (a note appears on the staff, and you name it or press the key on an on-screen keyboard), a reading drill, a fretboard drill, and ear quizzes for intervals and chords. A few minutes of flashcards a day is the fastest way to learn to read notes.

Each stage also gets an Anki deck. Anki is a free flashcard app that schedules reviews for you: a card you know comes back after a longer gap each time, and a card you miss comes back soon. Download a stage's deck from its overview page and import it (in Anki desktop, choose File, then Import; on Android, open it with AnkiDroid).

## Filming yourself

Many technique problems are easy to see on video and hard to feel while you play: a collapsed wrist, a thumb that sticks out, a strumming arm that stops moving. Lessons that teach a physical skill have a "Film yourself" checklist. Prop your phone up, record a minute, and compare with the list. Where seeing the motion helps, the lesson links one free video.

If anything hurts (hands, wrists, arms, shoulders or back), stop. Rest, check your posture against the lesson, and play shorter sessions. If pain keeps coming back, see a teacher or a doctor.
