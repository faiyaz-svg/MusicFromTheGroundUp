# Your daily practice

Thirty minutes a day is enough to get through this book, if the 30 minutes have a shape. Then you know what to do the moment you sit down. Every lesson ends with a routine built on the same shape, and this page explains it.

## The shape of a session

A session has four parts. Each lesson fills in the details; the minutes stay the same.

| Minutes | Part | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up | Slow, easy playing on the instrument you start with: a scale, a chord change, or last lesson's exercise |
| 15 | New skill | What this lesson teaches, in small pieces, hands apart before hands together |
| 5 | Sight reading or ear | Read a short line you have never seen, or play back a short melody you just heard |
| 5 | The other instrument | A short block on the piano if you started on the guitar, or the reverse |

The 5 minutes on the other instrument keeps both moving. It also rests the first hand and arm, which helps while you build calluses on the guitar or tire your forearms on the piano.

The lesson tag decides which instrument leads. A Guitar lesson means guitar for 20 minutes, then 5 minutes of piano. A Piano lesson means the reverse. For Theory, Reading and Ear lessons, alternate by day.

## A day with only 10 minutes

Some days are short. Keep the order and cut the middle:

1. Warm-up, 2 minutes, on either instrument.
2. New skill, 6 minutes, at a slow tempo, on the instrument the lesson names.
3. Play the hardest bar once, slowly, and stop.

Skip the other instrument and the sight reading that day. A 10-minute day still counts, and it keeps the habit going. If you have 3 minutes, play the new skill once and write one line in your log.

## Why short and daily

Spreading practice over several days beats the same total time in one block. Two lines of research point that way.

The first is spaced practice. Cepeda and colleagues combined 317 experiments and found that material studied in separate sessions is remembered longer than material studied in one sitting ([Cepeda et al., 2006, Psychological Bulletin](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)). Those experiments used word lists and facts, not finger movements. Treat the result as solid for note names, chord shapes and theory, and as a reasonable guess for technique.

The second is sleep. Walker and colleagues trained people on a finger-sequence typing task and tested them 12 hours later. People who slept in between got about 20% faster, and people who stayed awake did not improve ([Walker et al., 2002, Neuron](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12123620/)). A skill you practise today keeps improving overnight, so playing a little every day gives every session a night of sleep behind it.

## Slow practice and the metronome

Play new material slower than feels necessary. A slow tempo lets you hear each note and check each finger, and it stops you rehearsing a mistake at speed. A **metronome** is a device or app that clicks a steady beat at a tempo you set, measured in beats per minute (bpm).

Set the click, play the exercise, and listen for one thing: do your notes land on the clicks, or do they drift? When three clean run-throughs in a row feel easy, raise the tempo by 4 to 6 bpm. The [free apps and gear](free-apps-and-gear.md) page says where to get a metronome.

## Keep a practice log

After each session, write one line in a notebook: the date, the lesson and the tempo you reached.

| Date | Lesson | Tempo reached |
|---|---|---|
| Mar 3 | Treble staff, C position | 60 bpm |
| Mar 4 | Treble staff, C position | 66 bpm |

The log tells you where to start tomorrow. On a bad day it also shows that last week's tempo was lower than today's.

## When to move on

Each lesson ends with a "Move on when" box. It looks like this:

<div class="move-on" markdown>

### Move on when

- [ ] You can play the exercise three times in a row with no stops at 60 bpm
- [ ] You can name the notes without counting up from middle C

</div>

Tick a box when you can do it on an ordinary day, and a lucky best take does not count. If a box is still not ticked after a week, go back one lesson, or lower the target tempo by 10 bpm and build up again.

## Warm up, and stop if it hurts

Start every session with easy, slow playing. Shake out your hands and roll your shoulders first. Sit or stand with your shoulders down and your wrists level, not bent sharply up or down.

<div class="warning" markdown>

Pain is a stop sign. If a hand, wrist, arm, shoulder or back hurts, stop and rest. Sore fingertips on your fretting hand in the first weeks of guitar are normal; sharp or burning pain is not. If pain keeps coming back, see a teacher or a doctor.

</div>

## A sample week

This week alternates which instrument leads. Every day is 30 minutes except Sunday.

| Day | Leads | 20 minutes | 5 minutes on the other |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | Piano | Piano lesson | Guitar warm-up |
| Tuesday | Guitar | Guitar lesson | Piano warm-up |
| Wednesday | Piano | Piano lesson | Guitar warm-up |
| Thursday | Guitar | Guitar lesson | Piano warm-up |
| Friday | Piano | Piano lesson with sight reading | Guitar warm-up |
| Saturday | Guitar | Guitar lesson, then film yourself | Piano warm-up |
| Sunday | Either | 10-minute day: the week's hardest bar | None |

That is six full days and one short one, about 3 hours 10 minutes in the week.
