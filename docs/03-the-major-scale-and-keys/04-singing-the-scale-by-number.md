# Singing the scale by number

<span class="tag tag-ear">Ear</span>

After this lesson you can sing the notes 1 to 8 of a major scale over a held note, match a pitch you hear, and tell the sound of 1, 3, 5 and 8 from the notes in between.

Before this: [The major scale](02-the-major-scale.md), [The C major scale and thumb-under](03-c-major-scale-and-thumb-under.md). You need: your piano, and a quiet room.

## The idea

### Sound first: the drone

A **drone** is a note held steadily underneath other music. Play C3 (the C below middle C) and keep the key down, or hold it with the damper pedal, and sing the sound "ah" at a pitch that matches it. When your voice and the piano agree, the note seems to lock in place, and any slight difference makes a wobble you can hear. Matching a pitch like this is the base skill of this lesson.

Listen to the C major scale over a drone. The low C stays, and the scale climbs above it.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
%%score {RH LH}
V:RH clef=treble
V:LH clef=bass
[V:RH] C D E F | G A B c |]
[V:LH] C,4 | C,4 |]
```

### Home

The tonic is the note the scale starts on, and it is also the note the scale feels as home. Play the scale up to 7 and stop. The ear leans toward the next note, and waits. Play 8 and the lean ends. The tonic is not a louder note, only the one all the others lean toward.

Try this on the piano:

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
C D E F | G A B z |
C D E F | G A B c |]
```

The first line stops on 7 with a rest in place of 8. The second line completes the scale. You will probably hum the missing note while you listen to the first line, and that is the tonic pull.

### Singing by number

Sing the numbers as words: "one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight". Singing the number is slower than singing "la", but it ties the sound to the scale degree, and the number stays the same in every key. In the C major scale, 1 is C. In the G major scale, 1 is G. The sound of 5 against 1 is the same in both.

1. Play and hold the drone C3.
2. Sing "one" on C4 an octave above, matching it. If it is too high or too low for your voice, pick another drone note; any note can be the tonic.
3. Sing up: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, at a slow pace, one number for each beat at 60 bpm.
4. Check each number against the piano. Play the note, then sing it, then play it again to check.
5. Sing back down: eight, seven, six, five, four, three, two, one.

Common mistake at step 4: checking only your last note. Check the way you move between notes. If you drift upward, you will finish sharp.

If your voice breaks or goes strained, move to a lower note. Don't push. If your throat hurts, stop and rest.

### Four stable notes

Sing 1, then 3, then 5, then 8. In C major, those are the notes C, E, G and C. They are the notes of the C major chord: C major: C, E, G. Each sounds restful, like a place to stop. Compare them with 2, 4, 6 and 7, which sound like they want to move. The notes 7 and 2 lean to 8 and 1, 4 leans to 3, and 6 leans to 5.

You can hear the effect in tunes you know. Twinkle Twinkle (traditional) begins on 1 1 5 5 6 6 5, and the 6 pulls to the 5 at the end of the phrase. Ode to Joy (Beethoven) begins 3 3 4 5 5 4 3 2 1 1 2 3 3 2 2. The first phrase ends on 2, which leans toward 1, and the tune comes home to 1 only at the end of the second phrase.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=70
K:C
E E F G | G F E D | C C D E | E3/2 D/ D2 |]
```

Sing this as numbers first: three, three, four, five, five, four, three, two, one, one, two, three, three, two, two.

## Try it

Exercise 1. Play C, E and G together on the piano, then play D, F and A together. Which one sounds more like a place to stop?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

C, E and G. They are degrees 1, 3 and 5, the stable notes. D, F and A are degrees 2, 4 and 6, and they sound as if they want to move.

</details>

Exercise 2. You hear the scale 1 to 7 and it stops. Which number do you sing next to finish it?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Eight, the tonic an octave above.

</details>

Exercise 3. Sing the numbers 1 3 5 3 1 over a C drone, then check each note against the piano.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The notes are C, E, G, E, C. If your 3 was lower than the piano's E, you sang it flat. Sing it again, aiming a little higher.

</details>

### Practice routine (30 minutes)

| Minutes | What to do | Tempo | Listen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up: C major scale on the piano, each hand alone, with the fingering from lesson 3 | 60 bpm | Even notes |
| 10 | Sing: hold C3, sing 1 to 8 and back down by number. Check each note on the piano | 60 bpm | Your voice locking onto the drone |
| 5 | Sing 1 3 5 3 1 and 1 5 1 over the drone, then 1 2 3 2 1 | 60 bpm | The difference between steady and leaning notes |
| 5 | Sing Ode to Joy by number, then play it on the piano in C position | 70 bpm | The same numbers in your voice and under your fingers |
| 5 | Guitar: Em chord, one strum per bar, then strings 1 and 2 open, fret 1 and fret 3 | 60 bpm | Every string ringing |

Repeat this routine on at least three days.

## Common mistakes

Singing too high. Start in the middle of your voice, and pick a drone that suits it.

Drifting flat on the way down. Play the drone again and re-match it every second time.

Singing the notes as letter names. The numbers are the tool, and they carry over to every key.

## Key terms

- **Drone**: a note held steadily under other music.
- **Matching pitch**: singing the same pitch you hear.
- **Tonic**: the note the others lean toward, scale degree 1.
