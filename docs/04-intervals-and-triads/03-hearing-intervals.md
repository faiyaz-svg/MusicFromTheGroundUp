# Hearing intervals

<span class="tag tag-ear">Ear</span>

After this lesson you can name a minor 2nd, major 2nd, minor 3rd, major 3rd, perfect 4th, perfect 5th and octave by ear, using a tune you know for each one, and you can sing an interval above a note you are given.

Before this: [Interval numbers](01-interval-numbers.md), [Interval qualities](02-interval-qualities.md), [Singing the scale by number](../03-the-major-scale-and-keys/04-singing-the-scale-by-number.md). You need: your piano, your guitar and your voice.

## The idea

### Sound first: how wide is it

Play middle C and then the notes above it, one at a time, listening to the width. The 2nd is close, almost a neighbour. The 3rd is a short, easy move. The 4th and the 5th are wider and more open. The octave sounds like the same note again, higher.

Your ear learns an interval the way it learns a face: by meeting it many times and tying it to something familiar. For intervals, the familiar thing is the first two notes of a tune you know well.

### Reference tunes

A **reference tune** is a tune whose first two notes make a known interval. When you hear an unknown interval, you ask which tune it starts like. The tunes below are traditional or by a composer long dead, so they are free to use. Each bar of the example plays the interval, so you can check the tune against it.

| Interval | Direction | Reference tune | The two notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Major 2nd | Up | Frère Jacques (traditional) | C up to D |
| Major 2nd | Down | Mary Had a Little Lamb (traditional) | E down to D |
| Minor 2nd | Down | Für Elise (Beethoven) | E down to D# |
| Minor 3rd | Up | Greensleeves (traditional) | A up to C |
| Major 3rd | Up | When the Saints Go Marching In (traditional) | C up to E |
| Perfect 4th | Up | Amazing Grace (traditional) | D up to G |
| Perfect 5th | Up | Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star (traditional) | C up to G |

Listen to all seven in a row, then the octave. Each bar is the first interval of the tune named in the table, in the same order.

```abc
X:1
M:2/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=70
K:C
"^M2 up"C D | "^M2 down"E D | "^m2 down"e ^d | "^m3 up"A c |
"^M3 up"C E | "^P4 up"D G | "^P5 up"C G | "^octave"C c |]
```

Sing the first two notes of each tune, then play them on the piano to check. If your version of the tune sits in a different key, that is fine: the distance between the two notes is what counts. If the two notes you sing do not match the example, the way you remember the tune is not the way it goes, so choose another tune for that interval.

There is no common reference tune for the octave. Listen for "the same note, higher": the two notes blend so well they almost sound like one. Men and women singing the same song together usually sing an octave apart.

### Singing an interval

Singing is the fastest way to learn an interval, because your voice has to find the size. Follow these steps.

1. Play the lower note and sing it until your voice matches the key.
2. Hear the upper note in your head. Use the reference tune if you need it.
3. Sing the upper note.
4. Play the upper note on the piano to check. If your voice is lower or higher, adjust and try again.

Pick starting notes in a range where your voice is comfortable. If your throat feels tight or sore, stop and rest.

### Intervals on the guitar

Play the open string 1 (E) and then fret 5 on the same string (A). That is a perfect 4th, and it starts like Amazing Grace. Play the open string 1 and then fret 7 (B). That is a perfect 5th, like the first two notes of Twinkle, Twinkle. On a guitar you can find any of these by counting frets (lesson 2) and then listen for the tune.

## Try it: the self-test

Cover the staff with a sheet of paper, press play, and write down the interval you hear for each bar (quality and number). Then uncover the music and check.

Test 1: melodic intervals going up.

```abc
X:1
M:2/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=70
K:C
C G | D F | E A | G B |
A B | C c | B c | E G |]
```

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Perfect 5th, minor 3rd, perfect 4th, major 3rd, major 2nd, octave, minor 2nd, minor 3rd.

</details>

Test 2: harmonic intervals, with both notes together.

```abc
X:1
M:2/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=70
K:C
[EG]2 | [CF]2 | [DA]2 | [FG]2 |
[GB]2 | [Cc]2 |]
```

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Minor 3rd, perfect 4th, perfect 5th, major 2nd, major 3rd, octave.

</details>

Then use the [interval quiz](../practice/interval-quiz.md) in Practice tools. It plays an interval and you name it, and it scores you.

<div class="practice" markdown>

### Practice routine (30 minutes)

| Minutes | What to do | Tempo | Listen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up: play C up to each white key to the next C, singing the top note after you play it | 60 bpm | Your voice landing on the same pitch as the key |
| 15 | New skill: sing the first two notes of each reference tune, then play them to check; then play any note, pick an interval from the table, and sing the upper note before playing it | Untimed | The reference tune coming to mind before you answer |
| 5 | Ear training: the [interval quiz](../practice/interval-quiz.md), level 1, going up | Untimed | The width: close, medium or wide |
| 5 | Guitar: on string 1, play the open string with fret 3, 4, 5 and 7 in turn, naming each interval before you play it | 60 bpm | The tune each interval starts |

Repeat this routine on at least three days. Ear training grows in short daily sessions.

</div>

## Common mistakes

Reading the staff while you listen. The notation gives the answer away, so cover it and trust your ear first.

Trusting a tune from memory without checking it. Play the first two notes on the piano; if they are not the interval you expected, choose another tune for that interval.

Practising only going up. Do some of each direction, because many tunes begin with a downward move.

## Key terms

- **Reference tune**: a well-known tune whose first two notes make a particular interval, used to recognise that interval by ear.
- **Ear training**: practice in naming and singing what you hear.
