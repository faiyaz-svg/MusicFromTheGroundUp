# Hearing major and minor

<span class="tag tag-ear">Ear</span>

After this lesson you can tell by ear whether a chord, a scale, a short melody or a short chord progression is major or minor, at least 9 times out of 10.

Before this: [The natural minor scale](01-natural-minor.md), [Relative and parallel keys](05-relative-and-parallel-keys.md), [Hearing chord quality](../04-intervals-and-triads/12-hearing-chord-quality.md). You need: your piano and your guitar, and headphones if you can.

## The idea

### One note decides it

A major triad and a minor triad on the same root differ in one note: the 3rd. The C major triad is C, E, G. The C minor triad is C, Eb, G. The 3rd is four half steps above the root in a major chord and three in a minor chord. The same note decides a scale: the third note of the major scale is a major 3rd above the first, and the third note of any minor scale is a minor 3rd above the first.

Listen to the two chords in turn.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
[CEG]2 [C_EG]2 |]
```

Many listeners hear the first as bright and settled, and the second as darker or more serious. That is a tendency, not a law. Fast minor music can sound lively and slow major music can sound gentle. Use the colour as a clue, and confirm it with the steps below.

### Steps for finding it by ear

1. Find the home note. Hum along until one note feels like the place to rest. In a short example it is usually the last note or the lowest chord note.
2. Sing or hum up from the home note, one step at a time: 1, 2, 3. The third note is the one to compare.
3. Compare it with a major 3rd and a minor 3rd. Play C then E (major 3rd) and C then Eb (minor 3rd) on your piano. Does your sung 3rd match the wider or the narrower one?
4. For a chord, ignore the melody and ask only whether the middle note is wide (major) or narrow (minor).
5. For a progression, listen to the first and the last chords. The last chord is usually the home chord, and its quality gives the key.

Common mistake at step 1: taking the highest note for the home note. The home note is where the music feels finished, not where it climbs.

Songs help your ear hold the sound of each kind. Ode to Joy (Beethoven) and Twinkle Twinkle (traditional) are major. Greensleeves (traditional) is usually played in a minor key. Sing the first phrase of one and compare it with the 3rd you played a moment ago.

## Hear and see

The self-test has 10 items. Play each item once, say "major" or "minor", and then check the answers at the end. Replay an item as often as you need, but write down your first answer.

Items 1 to 4 are chords.

Item 1.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
[CEG]4 |]
```

Item 2.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
[DFA]4 |]
```

Item 3.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
[E^GB]4 |]
```

Item 4.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
[G_Bd]4 |]
```

Items 5 and 6 are scale fragments. Listen to the third note.

Item 5.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=70
K:C
C D _E F | G4 |]
```

Item 6.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=70
K:C
G A B c | d4 |]
```

Items 7 and 8 are short melodies. Find the home note, then the third.

Item 7.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=70
K:C
A c e c | B A ^G A |]
```

Item 8.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=70
K:G
G B d B | A G F G |]
```

Items 9 and 10 are chord progressions. Listen to where they come home.

Item 9.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
[CEG]2 [CEA]2 | [CFA]2 [B,DG]2 | [CEG]4 |]
```

Item 10.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
[A,CE]2 [DFA]2 | [E^GB]2 [A,CE]2 |]
```

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Major (C, E, G). 2. Minor (D minor: D, F, A). 3. Major (E major: E, G#, B). 4. Minor (G minor: G, Bb, D). 5. Minor (C, D, Eb: a minor 3rd). 6. Major (G, A, B: a major 3rd). 7. Minor (A minor, with the G# leading to A). 8. Major (G major). 9. Major (C, Am, F, G, C). 10. Minor (Am, Dm, E, Am).

</details>

## Try it

Exercise 1. Play any note on the piano. Then play the note a major 3rd above and a minor 3rd above. Do this on five different notes and describe each pair in a few words of your own.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Your words will differ. The major 3rd is four half steps (for C, the note E) and the minor 3rd is three (for C, the note Eb). The wider one usually sounds brighter.

</details>

Exercise 2. Play a G chord on the guitar and then a Gm chord shape on the piano (G, Bb, D). What is the changed note?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

B in the major chord becomes Bb in the minor chord. Only the 3rd changes.

</details>

Exercise 3. A friend plays the chords A and Am. Which one has C#, and which has C?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A major has C# (A, C#, E). A minor has C (A, C, E).

</details>

## Common mistakes

Deciding by speed or volume. The test is the 3rd, not whether the music sounds sad or happy.

Not finding the home note. A melody that starts on its 3rd or 5th can fool you. Wait for where it comes to rest.

Guessing from the first chord only. A progression can start on a minor chord and end major. Listen to the last chord.

## Key terms

- **Major 3rd**: an interval of four half steps; it makes a chord or scale major.
- **Minor 3rd**: an interval of three half steps; it makes a chord or scale minor.
- **Home note**: the tonic, the note where a piece feels at rest.
