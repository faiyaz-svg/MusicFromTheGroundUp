# Sight-reading in new keys

<span class="tag tag-reading">Reading</span>

After this lesson you can scan a new piece in G, D, F or a minor key, find the notes the key signature changes and the accidentals, and play four bars at sight without stopping.

Before this: [Sight-reading basics](../02-two-hands-and-first-chords/09-sight-reading-basics.md), [Key signatures with sharps](../03-the-major-scale-and-keys/07-sharp-key-signatures.md), [Flat keys and the circle of fifths](../03-the-major-scale-and-keys/09-flat-keys-and-the-circle-of-fifths.md), [Relative and parallel keys](05-relative-and-parallel-keys.md), [Harmonic and melodic minor](02-harmonic-and-melodic-minor.md). You need: your piano.

## The idea

### Why a new key changes sight reading

In C major every white key is a note of the key, and you read by shape. In G, D, F and the minor keys, some notes are sharp or flat all the way through. If you read the letter and play the white key, you hit a wrong note on every F in G major. Good sight readers do the key work before they play, so their hands know it in advance.

### The scan

Spend 20 to 30 seconds on this before you play. Steps 1 to 4 are new; steps 5 and 6 repeat the scan from stage 2.

1. Read the key signature and name the key. One sharp is G major or E minor. Two sharps is D major or B minor. One flat is F major or D minor.
2. Mark the notes the signature changes. On a printed page, circle each one: every F in G major, every F and C in D major, every B in F major.
3. Decide major or minor. Look at the last note and the first chord. In a minor key, look for an accidental that is the raised 7th note (G# in A minor, D# in E minor, C# in D minor). It leads up to the tonic, and it is not part of the key signature.
4. Scan for all other accidentals and note the bar where each occurs. An accidental holds until the bar line.
5. Check the time signature and the shape of the rhythm.
6. Find the highest and lowest notes and the biggest leap.

You then play at a tempo slow enough to keep the beat steady, and you do not stop for mistakes.

| Key | Key signature | Notes to change | Raised 7th (minor keys) |
|---|---|---|---|
| G major | one sharp | every F is F# | none |
| D major | two sharps | every F is F#, every C is C# | none |
| F major | one flat | every B is Bb | none |
| A minor | none | none | G# |
| E minor | one sharp | every F is F# | D# |
| D minor | one flat | every B is Bb | C# |

## Hear and see

Each tune has four bars. Do the scan, then play it at 50 bpm without stopping. Write the answers to the scan questions before you look at them. The answers for all six are in the box below the last one.

Tune 6.

```abc

K:Dm
D F A F | G E ^C D | F A d B | A ^C D2 |]
```

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answers to the scan</summary>

1. G major, one sharp. The F in bar 2 is F#.
2. D major, two sharps. The F's are F# in bars 1, 3 and 4, and the C in bar 2 is C#.
3. F major, one flat. The B in bar 2 is Bb.
4. A minor, no key signature. The accidental G# appears in bars 2 and 4 and leads up to A.
5. E minor, one sharp. The F in bar 2 is F#, and the accidental D# appears in bars 2 and 4 and leads up to E.
6. D minor, one flat. The B in bar 3 is Bb. The accidental C# appears in bars 2 and 4 and leads up to D.

</details>

## Try it

Exercise 1. A piece has two sharps and ends on B. Which key is it likely to be?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

B minor. Two sharps could be D major or B minor, and the final note B points to B minor.

</details>

Exercise 2. A piece has no key signature, but a G# keeps appearing and the last note is A. Which key?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A minor. The G# is the raised 7th note (the leading tone), written as an accidental.

</details>

Exercise 3. In F major, which notes does the key signature change, and how?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Every B becomes Bb.

</details>

<div class="practice" markdown>

### Practice routine (30 minutes)

| Minutes | What to do | Tempo | Listen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up: G major and A minor scales, right hand, one octave | 60 bpm | Even notes |
| 15 | New skill: for each of the six tunes, scan for 30 seconds, write the key and the changed notes, then play without stopping | 50 bpm | The changed notes (F#, Bb, G#) played correctly the first time |
| 5 | Reading: one level on the [reading drill](../practice/reading-drill.md) | Untimed | Keeping going after a wrong note |
| 5 | Guitar: the Am, Em, Dm and G chords, one strum per bar | 60 bpm | Clean changes |

Repeat this routine on at least three days. Use the same six tunes the first day, then pick new music from your own books.

</div>

### Move on when

- [ ] You can name the key (or the two possible keys) and the notes it changes for tunes 1 to 6 in under 30 seconds each.
- [ ] You can play any one of tunes 1 to 6 at 50 bpm, without stopping, on your first read.
- [ ] You can play tunes 1 to 6 at 60 bpm, three times in a row with no stops.
- [ ] You can find the raised 7th note in tunes 4 to 6 and say which note it leads to.

## Common mistakes

Reading the key signature and then forgetting it. Circle the changed notes first.

Missing an accidental that came earlier in the bar. Accidentals hold to the bar line, so look back along the bar.

Stopping at a wrong note. Keep the beat and play the next note correctly; fixing it later is what practice is for.

## Key terms

- **Sight-reading**: playing music you have not seen before.
- **Raised 7th**: the accidental that raises the 7th note of a minor key by a half step to make a leading tone.
