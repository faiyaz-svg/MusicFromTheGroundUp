# Triplets and syncopation

<span class="tag tag-theory">Theory</span>

After this lesson you can count and clap eighth-note triplets, spot a syncopated rhythm on the page, and count one with ties across the beat.

Before this: [Compound meter](01-compound-meter.md), [Ties, dots and eighth notes](../02-two-hands-and-first-chords/05-ties-dots-and-eighth-notes.md). You need: your piano and a metronome app.

## The idea

### Triplets: three in the time of two

In 4/4, a quarter-note beat usually splits into two eighth notes. A **triplet** squeezes three notes of the same value into the time of two. An eighth-note triplet puts three even notes into one quarter-note beat.

You write it with a small 3 and a bracket or beam over the three notes. Count it "1 trip let 2 trip let", one syllable for each note.

Listen to two beats of straight eighth notes, then two beats of triplets, then the same again. The bar with triplets has more notes in the same time.

```abc
X:1
M:2/4
L:1/8
Q:1/4=60
K:C
C D E F | (3CDE (3FGA | C D E F | (3CDE (3FGA |]
```

This is in 2/4, two beats to a bar. Bar 1 holds four eighth notes, two for each beat. Bar 2 holds two triplets, one for each beat, so it has six notes in the same time. In ABC the first triplet is written `(3CDE`.

Triplets and 6/8 both put three notes in a beat. The difference is the meter: in 4/4 a triplet is a special group inside a beat that normally splits in two, while in 6/8 every beat splits in three, so no bracket is needed.

### Syncopation: stress on the off-beat

In 4/4 the strong beats are 1 and 3, and beats 2 and 4 are a little lighter. The "&" between the numbers is the weakest place. **Syncopation** moves the stress to a weak place: notes start on the off-beat ("&") and are held across the next beat, so the strong beat has no new note.

Listen to the next example. The notes start on the "&" of each beat and last two eighth notes, so you never hear a note start on a strong beat except the first.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/8
Q:1/4=60
K:C
E F2 G2 A2 G | F E2 D2 E2 D | C8 |]
```

Count it "1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &". In bar 1, E starts on 1. F starts on the "&" after 1 and lasts through the start of beat 2, G starts on the "&" after 2 and lasts through beat 3, and so on. Tap your foot on every number as you listen. Your foot lands in the middle of the long notes, and that tension is what syncopation sounds like.

### Ties make syncopation

A **tie** joins two notes of the same pitch into one longer note. When the second note of a tie starts on a strong beat (or in the next bar), you hear one note start on a weak place and last through the strong one.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/8
Q:1/4=60
K:C
C2 D2 E2 z F- | F2 E2 D2 C2 | C8 |]
```

Bar 1 ends with F on the "&" of 4. That F is tied across the bar line, so it sounds through beat 1 of bar 2 and you hear no new F on that beat. Count it "1 & 2 & 3 & 4 (and)" and let the F ring.

Watch out: a tie joins two notes of the same pitch. A curved line over two different pitches is a slur (legato), not a tie.

### Counting syncopated rhythms

Follow these steps.

1. Write "1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &" under the bar, one count for each eighth note.
2. Mark which counts start a note. Everything else is held or rest.
3. Say all the counts aloud and clap only on the counts that start a note.
4. Tap your foot on 1 2 3 4 the whole time.

Common mistake at step 4: stopping the foot when the clap lands off the beat. The foot keeps going; it is the clap that moves.

Exercise 1. How many notes are in a bar made of four eighth-note triplets, and how many beats do they last?

Twelve notes (4 triplets times 3), lasting four beats.

Exercise 2. In this bar, on which counts does a note start?

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/8
K:C
c d2 e2 f2 g |]
```

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

On 1, then on the "&" after 1, the "&" after 2, the "&" after 3 and the "&" after 4. The bar has five notes. The three long ones start on off-beats and cover the beats 2, 3 and 4, so no new note starts on those beats.

## Common mistakes

Rushing the triplets into a "1 and a 2 and a" lilt. The three notes of a triplet are equally spaced; say "trip let" evenly with a slow metronome.

Playing the tied note again. After a tie there is no second attack. Hold the first note through the full length of both.

Putting the clap on the beat. In syncopation the clap lands off the beat; the foot stays on it.

## Key terms

- **Triplet**: three notes of the same value played in the time of two.
- **Syncopation**: stress on a weak beat or the off-beat, with the strong beat tied or silent.
- **Off-beat**: the "&" between counted beats.
- **Tie**: a curved line joining two notes of the same pitch into one.
