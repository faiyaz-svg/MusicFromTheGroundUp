# Voicing the melody

<span class="tag tag-piano">Piano</span>

After this lesson you can make the melody louder than the accompaniment, both when the melody and accompaniment are in different hands and when they share one hand.

## The idea

### Sound first: all the notes at once

Play the first example as it is written. Every note has the same loudness, and the tune is hard to pick out of the left-hand pattern. Then play the second example, which has the same notes. Now the right hand is louder and the left hand is quiet, and the tune stands out.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/8
Q:1/4=66
K:C

V:RH clef=treble
V:LH clef=bass
[V:RH] !mf!e4 g4 | a4 g4 |]
[V:LH] !mf!C,G,E,G, C,G,E,G, | F,,C,A,,C, G,,D,B,,D, |]
```

```abc
K:C
%%score {RH LH}
V:RH clef=treble
V:LH clef=bass
[V:RH] !mf!e4 g4 | a4 g4 |]
[V:LH] !pp!C,G,E,G, C,G,E,G, | F,,C,A,,C, G,,D,B,,D, |]
```

Making one line louder than the others is called **voicing**. A pianist decides which notes sing and which sit in the background, and plays them at different volumes.

### Why voicing matters

A piano has one keyboard but music often has two or three layers: a melody, a bass and a pattern between. If all the layers are the same loudness, the listener cannot follow the tune. Voicing makes the layers clear.

The rule is simple: the melody is the loudest layer, the bass is next, and the middle pattern is the quietest. In this lesson we practise the first step, melody over accompaniment.

### Voicing across hands

When the melody is in the right hand and the accompaniment is in the left, give the hands different weights.

1. Play the left hand alone at p, listening to it.
2. Play the right hand alone at mf, listening to it.
3. Play them together, and listen: the right hand should be audibly louder.
4. If the left hand covers the melody, play it even softer. The gap between the hands should be a clear step, not a hair.

Your digital piano turns key speed into volume (touch sensitivity). A slow key press is soft and a faster one is louder, so the difference is in how fast you press, not in how hard you hold the key. Keep the left-hand fingers close to the keys and move them slowly for the pattern.

Common mistake at step 4: lifting the whole left hand to be quiet. It makes the pattern uneven. Keep the fingers close and press gently.

### Voicing inside one hand

Sometimes the melody and the accompaniment are in the same hand. Here the melody is the top note of a chord in the right hand, with a lower note held beneath it.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=66
K:C
%%score {(RH RH2) LH}
V:RH clef=treble
V:RH2 clef=treble
V:LH clef=bass
[V:RH] !mf!G2 A2 | G4 | E2 F2 | E4 |]
[V:RH2] !pp![CE]4 | [CE]4 | [B,D]4 | [G,C]4 |]
[V:LH] !p!C,,4 | C,,4 | G,,4 | C,,4 |]
```

The top note is played by finger 5 in the right hand. To bring it out:

1. Place the hand on the chord with your arm weight resting on finger 5.
2. Press the keys under the other fingers very lightly, so they sound but do not stick out.
3. Check by playing only the top note, then all the notes: the top note should sound the same in both.
4. Record yourself and listen. If you can hear the lower notes as loudly as the top, press them more lightly.

Common mistake at step 2: playing all the fingers with equal weight. The melody note then gets lost. Put the weight on the finger that plays the melody.

If a hand, wrist or arm aches, stop and rest. If it keeps happening, see a teacher or a doctor.

## Common mistakes

Playing everything at one loudness. The tune disappears. Decide which layer sings.

Making the left hand quiet by lifting it. The pattern gets uneven. Press gently with fingers close to the keys.

Pressing all five fingers equally in a chord. Put the weight on the melody finger.

## Key terms

- **Voicing**: making one line of the music louder than the others.
- **Melody**: the line the listener follows; the loudest layer.
- **Accompaniment**: the quieter layers that support the melody.
