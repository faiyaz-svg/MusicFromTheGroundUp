# Hearing intervals 2

<span class="tag tag-ear">Ear</span>

After this lesson you can hear and name major and minor 6ths and 7ths and the tritone, say what a compound interval is, and recognize these intervals by their sound and by their inversions.

Before this: [Interval qualities](../04-intervals-and-triads/02-interval-qualities.md), [Hearing intervals](../04-intervals-and-triads/03-hearing-intervals.md), [Hearing major and minor](../05-minor-keys-and-chord-charts/08-hearing-major-and-minor.md). You need: your piano or guitar, and your ears.

## The idea

### The wide intervals

In stage 4 you learned to hear 2nds, 3rds, 4ths, 5ths and octaves. This lesson adds the wide ones. An interval is the distance between two notes, counted in letter names; its quality (major, minor, perfect, augmented, diminished) says how many half steps it holds.

| Interval | Half steps | From C | What it sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minor 6th | 8 | C up to Ab | Yearning and open |
| Major 6th | 9 | C up to A | Warm and bright |
| Minor 7th | 10 | C up to Bb | Unfinished, leaning forward |
| Major 7th | 11 | C up to B | Tense, almost an octave |
| Tritone | 6 | C up to F# | Restless and unstable |

C up to Ab is a minor 6th. C up to A is a major 6th. C up to Bb is a minor 7th. C up to B is a major 7th. C up to F# is an augmented 4th, which sounds the same as C up to Gb, a diminished 5th; the name **tritone** covers both, because the distance is three whole tones (6 half steps).

### Hearing them by inversion

A wide interval is a small one turned upside down. If you move the lower note up an octave, the interval inverts, and the two numbers add up to 9.

| Wide interval | Upside down | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Major 6th | Minor 3rd | C up to A inverts A up to C |
| Minor 6th | Major 3rd | C up to Ab inverts Ab up to C |
| Minor 7th | Major 2nd | C up to Bb inverts Bb up to C |
| Major 7th | Minor 2nd | C up to B inverts B up to C |
| Tritone | Tritone | C up to F# inverts F# up to C |

So the sound of a major 7th is the sound of a minor 2nd spread wide: it grates, and wants to resolve up to the octave. A minor 7th is a major 2nd spread wide: it is softer. The tritone is the only interval that is its own inversion, which is why it sounds the same either way up. A dominant 7th chord such as G7 (G, B, D, F) has both: G up to F is a minor 7th, and B up to F is a tritone.

### Reference tunes and singing

A reference tune is a song you know whose first two notes are the interval. For the major 6th, the traditional tune My Bonnie Lies over the Ocean starts with it going up (G up to E). Singing the interval helps more than naming it: play the lower note, sing the higher, then play it to check.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=70
K:C
G e |]
```

You do not need a tune for every interval. Use the inversion trick: if a 6th sounds like a wide version of a 3rd you know, work out which 3rd.

### Compound intervals

A **compound interval** is larger than an octave. It is named by adding 7 to the simple interval: a 2nd plus an octave is a 9th, a 3rd plus an octave is a 10th, and a 5th plus an octave is a 12th. C4 up to the D above the next octave is a major 9th: 14 half steps, an octave (12) and a major 2nd (2). C4 up to the E in the next octave is a major 10th. To name a compound interval, subtract 7 from the number: a 9th sounds like a 2nd with a gap, a 10th like a 3rd.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/2
Q:1/4=60
K:C
C d | [Cd]2 |]
```

## Common mistakes

Confusing a major 7th with an octave. A major 7th sounds nearly right and wants to move; the octave is calm.

Naming by the quality alone. Always count the letters first (C up to A is a 6th), then listen for major or minor.

Ignoring the lower note when singing. Sing from the lower note each time.

## Key terms

- **Tritone**: an interval of six half steps (augmented 4th or diminished 5th).
- **Compound interval**: an interval larger than an octave.
- **Inversion of an interval**: moving the lower note up an octave; the two interval numbers add up to 9.
