# The chords of a key

<span class="tag tag-theory">Theory</span>

After this lesson you can say what job each chord of a key does (tonic, subdominant or dominant), name the common progressions I IV V I, I vi IV V and ii V I with numerals, and play them in C and in G.

Before this: [Roman numerals](01-roman-numerals.md), [Open seventh chords](../04-intervals-and-triads/06-open-seventh-chords.md), [Chords from a lead sheet on piano](../05-minor-keys-and-chord-charts/06-chords-from-a-lead-sheet-on-piano.md). You need: your piano and your guitar.

## The idea

### Three jobs

The seven chords of a key are not equal. Play C, then G, then C on the piano. The first C feels like home, the G feels like a step away that wants to come back, and the last C settles it. Now play C, F, C. The F is also away from home, but more gently than G.

Chords in a key do one of three jobs, called **harmonic functions**.

| Function | Chords in C major | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Tonic | I (C), also vi (Am) and iii (Em) | Home. Rest. A place to start and to end. |
| Subdominant | IV (F), also ii (Dm) | Leaves home. Mild tension. |
| Dominant | V (G), also vii° (Bdim) | Strong tension. Wants to return to the tonic. |

The names come from the scale. The **tonic** is the chord on the first note. The **dominant** is the chord on the fifth note. The **subdominant** is the chord on the note a 5th below the tonic, which is the fourth note of the scale (F is a 5th below C).

Why do vi and iii count as tonic chords? They share two notes with I. C major is C, E, G and Am is A, C, E, so they share C and E. Em is E, G, B and shares E and G with C. In the same way, ii (D, F, A) shares F and A with IV (F, A, C), and vii° (B, D, F) shares B and D with V (G, B, D).

### The usual path

Most progressions in a major key follow one path: tonic, then subdominant, then dominant, then back to the tonic. Each stage can be skipped, but going back the other way (dominant to subdominant) is much rarer in classical music. Here is the path with the simplest chords, I IV V I, played with a right-hand triad and a left-hand root.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
%%score {RH LH}
V:RH clef=treble
V:LH clef=bass
[V:RH] "C"[CEG]4 | "F"[CFA]4 | "G"[B,DG]4 | "C"[CEG]4 |]
[V:LH] C,4 | F,4 | G,4 | C,4 |]
```

Each chord moves to the next by the nearest inversion, as in stage 4. The right hand only steps.

### Three common progressions

Progression 1 is **I IV V I**, above. It has all three functions and is the basic shape of many songs.

Progression 2 is **I vi IV V**. The vi chord (Am) is a tonic chord, so the first two bars both sit at home, with a change of colour. The IV then leaves, and V pulls back. Listen to how the Am softens the opening.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
%%score {RH LH}
V:RH clef=treble
V:LH clef=bass
[V:RH] "C"[CEG]4 | "Am"[CEA]4 | "F"[CFA]4 | "G"[B,DG]4 |]
[V:LH] C,4 | A,,4 | F,,4 | G,,4 |]
```

Progression 3 is **ii V I**. The ii is a subdominant chord, V is the dominant, and I is home. The roots move down by a 5th each time (D to G to C), which makes this the smoothest way to get home. Listen to the last chord arrive.

```abc
X:1
M:3/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
%%score {RH LH}
V:RH clef=treble
V:LH clef=bass
[V:RH] "Dm"[DFA]3 | "G"[DGB]3 | "C"[EGc]3 |]
[V:LH] D,3 | G,,3 | C,3 |]
```

Stage 5 introduced the loop I V vi IV (C G Am F). It uses the same four chords as progression 2, in a different order, and it is a very common loop in pop music. Its order is tonic, dominant, tonic, subdominant.

### The same progressions in other keys

The numerals do not change when the key changes. Only the chord names do. Spell the three progressions in four keys.

| Key | I IV V I | I vi IV V | ii V I |
|---|---|---|---|
| C major | C, F, G, C | C, Am, F, G | Dm, G, C |
| G major | G, C, D, G | G, Em, C, D | Am, D, G |
| F major | F, Bb, C, F | F, Dm, Bb, C | Gm, C, F |
| D major | D, G, A, D | D, Bm, G, A | Em, A, D |

Check one entry by building the chord. In G major, vi is built on E, and it is Em: E, G, B. In D major, ii is built on E, and it is also Em: E, G, B.

### On the guitar

Open chords make the C version easy. Play C, Am, F and G for progression 2, and Dm, G and C for progression 3. In G major, play G, Em, C and D. Strum four down strokes in each bar and change on beat 1. The F is the barre chord from stage 8.

Listen for the pull. After G (the dominant), the C chord should sound like an answer. If you play G and stop, the progression sounds unfinished.

## Try it

Exercise 1. Label G, Em, C, D in the key of G with numerals and functions.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

I (tonic), vi (tonic), IV (subdominant), V (dominant).

</details>

Exercise 2. Spell the three chords of ii V I in the key of F.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

ii is Gm (G, Bb, D), V is C (C, E, G) and I is F (F, A, C).

</details>

Exercise 3. In C major, which chords share two notes with the dominant chord G?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Bdim (B, D, F) shares B and D. Em (E, G, B) also shares G and B, but Em is a tonic chord. The dominant function belongs to V and vii°.

</details>

### Practice routine (30 minutes)

| Minutes | What to do | Tempo | Listen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up: C major and G major scales, hands separately | 60 bpm | An even thumb crossing |
| 15 | New skill: I IV V I, I vi IV V and ii V I in C, then in G (right-hand triad, left-hand root), calling out the numerals | 50 bpm, then 60 | The nearest inversion, with only steps in the right hand |
| 5 | Ear training: play I IV V and stop on V, then play I. Hear the dominant ask and the tonic answer | Untimed | The unfinished feeling on V |
| 5 | Guitar: C Am F G and Dm G C, four strums per bar | 60 bpm | A clean change on beat 1 |

Repeat this routine on at least three days.

## Common mistakes

Thinking the numerals are fixed chords. IV is F in C major and C in G major. Always say the key first.

Treating vi as a different kind of chord from I. It is a minor chord, but it shares two notes with I and does the same job (home), with a darker colour.

Playing the dominant long and the tonic short. Hold the V for the full bar and let the I ring, so you can hear the arrival.

## Key terms

- **Harmonic function**: the job a chord does in a key: tonic, subdominant or dominant.
- **Tonic**: the home chord, I (or i in minor), with vi and iii as substitutes.
- **Dominant**: the chord on the 5th note of the scale (V), which pulls toward the tonic.
- **Subdominant**: the chord on the 4th note of the scale (IV), with ii as a substitute.
