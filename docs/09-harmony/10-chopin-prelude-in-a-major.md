## The idea

### The piece

A **prelude** is a short piece for piano that does not follow a set form. Frédéric Chopin wrote 24 preludes as his Opus 28, one in each major and minor key. This is No. 7, in A major. It has 16 bars. The score marks it Andantino (a walking tempo, near andante) and dolce e semplice, which means sweetly and plainly.

It is in 3/4 time, so each bar has three quarter-note beats, as in the Minuet in G. A major has three sharps, which are F#, C# and G#, so every F, C and G in the score is sharp unless a sign says otherwise.

### Phrases and rhythm

The piece is made of eight **phrases** of two bars each (a phrase is a musical sentence, as in the Minuet lesson). Bars 1 and 2 are the first phrase, bars 3 and 4 the second, and so on to bars 15 and 16. Every phrase has the same rhythm.

The first bar of a phrase has a long-short figure on beat 1, then two quarter notes. The figure is a dotted eighth note (an eighth note plus half its length, three sixteenths) followed by a sixteenth note, so together they fill one beat. The second bar has a half note and a quarter note. The quarter note on beat 3 of that bar leads into the next phrase, so the music is always moving forward. The left hand keeps one pattern too: a low bass note on beat 1, then a chord on beats 2 and 3.

The piece starts with a **pickup** (also called an anacrusis): a short bar before bar 1, here one quarter note E, that leads into the first full bar. It ends on a two-beat chord followed by a rest.

### The chords

Here are the chords, bar by bar, with their Roman numerals in A major. The notes are taken from the score.

| Bars | Chord | Numeral | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 and 2 | E7 | V7 | E, G#, B, D |
| 3 and 4 | A | I | A, C#, E |
| 5 and 6 | E (with the 7th, D) | V | E in the bass, with B, D and F# above |
| 7 and 8 | A | I | A, C#, E |
| 9 and 10 | E7 | V7 | E, G#, B, D |
| 11 | A | I | A, C#, E |
| 12 | F#7 | V7 of ii | F#, A#, C#, E |
| 13 | Bm (then Bm7) | ii | B, D, F# (and A) |
| 14 | E7 | V7 | E, G#, B, D |
| 15 and 16 | A | I | A, C#, E |

The bass tells you most of this: E (the dominant) in bars 1 to 2, 5 to 6, 9 to 10 and 14, and A (the tonic) in bars 3 to 4, 7 to 8, 11, 15 and 16. The piece goes from V to I over and over, like asking and answering.

Bar 12 is the only bar with a chord from outside the key. F#7 has A# in it, which is not in A major. F#7 is the dominant 7th chord of B, the chord that follows it. A V7 chord that leads to a chord of the key other than the tonic is a **secondary dominant**. Here F#7 is the V7 of B, so it leans toward Bm (ii), then Bm leads to E7 (V7), and E7 leads home to A. This ii to V7 to I path is the one from the lessons on the chords of a key.

Bars 15 and 16 end the piece with a **perfect cadence**: V7 in bar 14, then A in bars 15 and 16 with both chords in root position, and the melody ending on A, the tonic.

### Notes outside the chords

Some notes are not in the chord, and they are the flavour of the piece. In bar 3, beat 1, the right hand plays B# and D# above the A bass. They are non-chord tones (B# is the same key as C). They are reached by a leap down from the notes before, and they step up a half step to C# and E on the next sixteenth, so they are appoggiaturas, as in the previous lesson, played as a pair. The same happens in bar 11. In bar 7, beat 1, the bass moves to A while the right hand still plays D and G#, the notes of the E7 chord before. Against A they are non-chord tones, held over from the old chord and resolving by step to C# and A, so they act like suspensions.

### Pedalling

The score has no pedal marks. Use the damper pedal as in stage 7, as a suggestion, not as Chopin's instruction.

1. Press the pedal right after the bass note of beat 1 has sounded.
2. On beat 1 of the next bar, lift the pedal as you play the new bass note and press it again right after, so each bar starts with a clear pedal.
3. In bars 3, 7, 11 and 15 the bass moves from E to A. Change the pedal exactly as the A sounds. If the old pedal is still down, E and A blur together.
4. In every even bar the left hand rests on beat 3, so only the right hand sounds. Keep the pedal down through that rest unless the melody blurs.

Listen for blur, and change the pedal more often if the melody smudges.

### The hard spot: the wide chord in bar 12

Bar 12 has the widest chord of the piece. The right hand plays five notes on beat 1: A#4, C#5, E5, A#5 and C#6. That is a 10th from top to bottom, and most hands cannot play it as a block. The left hand plays F#3, C#4, E4 and F#4 under it.

Here are bars 11 and 12 as an exercise. Bar 11 has the held notes C#5 and E5 with A5 and C#6 above on beats 2 and 3.

```abc
X:1
M:3/4
L:1/16
Q:1/4=60
K:A
%%score {(RH RH2) LH}
V:RH clef=treble
V:RH2 clef=treble
V:LH clef=bass
V:RH
[^B^d]3[ce]8- [ce] | [^Ace^ac']8 [Ac]4 |]
V:RH2
x4 [ac']4 [ac']4 | x12 |]
V:LH
A,,4 [A,EA]4 [A,EA]4 | [F,CEF]8 z4 |]
```

Follow these steps.

1. Play the right-hand chord one note at a time, from the bottom up, to find the notes: A#4, C#5, E5, A#5, C#6.
2. Choose a fingering that fits your hand, such as 1, 2 and 3 on the lower three (A#4, C#5, E5) and 3 and 5 on A#5 and C#6, and write it on the score.
3. Join them: **roll** the chord, which means playing the notes one after another very fast from the bottom, with the pedal down. The notes ring together like a chord.
4. Add the left hand, which plays F#3 to F#4 as a four-note chord, with the roll starting at the same time as the bass.

Common mistake at step 3: rolling too slowly. The roll should be quick, and end on the top note in time with beat 1.

### Plan for learning

Learn the piece in four sections, hands separately first, then together.

| Section | Bars | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pickup, 1 to 4 | The melody and chords set the pattern for the whole piece |
| 2 | 5 to 8 | The right hand holds a low D, then plays the melody over it |
| 3 | 9 to 11 | Like section 1, with fuller chords |
| 4 | 12 to 16 | The wide chord and the ending |

Start at 60 bpm. After three clean plays of each section, join the sections two at a time.

## Common mistakes

Rushing the long-short figure. Count the dotted eighth as three sixteenths and the next note as one.

Pedalling through the bass change. The pedal from the old bass sustains the E into the A. Lift the pedal as the bass key goes down.

Playing the chords louder than the melody. The melody is the top note.

## Key terms

- **Prelude**: a short piano piece without a set form.
- **Secondary dominant**: a V7 chord that leads to a chord in the key other than the tonic (F#7 leading to Bm).
- **Roll** (an arpeggiated chord): playing a chord's notes quickly from the bottom up, so that they ring together.
