# Hearing chord progressions 2

<span class="tag tag-ear">Ear</span>

After this lesson you can recognise I IV V I, I vi IV V, I V vi IV and ii V I by ear, name a cadence from its last two chords, and write down the Roman numerals of a short progression you hear.

Before this: [The chords of a key](02-diatonic-chords.md), [Cadences](03-cadences.md), [Hearing chord progressions](../07-pedal-texture-and-ambient/08-hearing-progressions.md), [Hearing chord quality](../04-intervals-and-triads/12-hearing-chord-quality.md). You need: your piano, your guitar and a sheet of paper to cover the staff.

## The idea

### Three questions to ask

When you hear a progression, you do not need to name each chord at once. Ask three questions in order.

1. Where is home? Find the tonic first. Hum the note that sounds like the end of the music and play it on the piano, then call it 1.
2. Where does the bass go? The bass note is the easiest thing to hear. Sing each bass note as a scale number (1, 4, 5, 6, and so on) over the home note, as in stage 3.
3. Is each chord major or minor? You trained this with triads in stage 4, and the lesson on the chords of a key told you which scale degrees are minor (2, 3 and 6).

Then write the numerals. The bass numbers give the root, and major or minor gives the case.

### Four fingerprints

Each progression has a pattern in its bass line that you can learn to recognise. This table shows the bass notes in C major and the movement between them.

| Progression | Chords in C | Bass notes | Bass movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| I IV V I | C, F, G, C | C, F, G, C | up a 4th, up a step, up a 4th (or down a 5th) |
| I vi IV V | C, Am, F, G | C, A, F, G | down a 3rd, down a 3rd, up a step |
| I V vi IV | C, G, Am, F | C, G, A, F | down a 4th, up a step, down a 3rd |
| ii V I | Dm, G, C | D, G, C | up a 4th, up a 4th (or down a 5th each time) |

Listen to the first two. In the I IV V I progression the bass climbs to the dominant and drops back with a sense of arrival. In I vi IV V it falls in 3rds and then climbs a step to V, which asks for the first chord again.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=60
K:C
%%score {RH LH}
V:RH clef=treble
V:LH clef=bass
[V:RH] [CEG]4 | [CFA]4 | [B,DG]4 | [CEG]4 |
[V:LH] C,4 | F,4 | G,4 | C,4 |
[V:RH] [CEG]4 | [CEA]4 | [CFA]4 | [B,DG]4 |]
[V:LH] C,4 | A,,4 | F,,4 | G,,4 |]
```

The I V vi IV loop starts the same way as I IV V I on the first two chords, so the third chord decides. If the bass falls to a minor chord, it is vi.

Listen to the cadences once more. A phrase ends on a chord that sounds finished (perfect or plagal), open (half) or surprised (deceptive). Ask yourself whether the last chord feels like home.

K:C
%%score {RH LH}
V:RH clef=treble

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

### Practice routine (30 minutes)

| Minutes | What to do | Tempo | Listen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up: play the tonic, then sing 1 4 5 1 over it, in C and in G | 60 bpm | Landing on 1 |
| 15 | New skill: play each of the four progressions in C on the piano, singing the bass notes as numbers; then the three self-tests | 60 bpm | The shape of the bass |
| 5 | Ear training: the [chord quiz](../practice/chord-quiz.md), then a friend (or a recording of a song you know) stops on a chord and you say if it is home | Untimed | Home or away |
| 5 | Guitar: C, F, G, C and then C, Am, F, G, naming the numerals as you strum | 60 bpm | The bass note of each chord |

Repeat this routine on at least three days.

## Common mistakes

Listening to the top note instead of the bass. The top note often stays the same across several chords. Follow the lowest note.

Guessing from the notation. In the self-tests the staff gives the answer away, so cover it first.

Calling every dark chord vi. Check the bass number: vi has bass 6, ii has bass 2 and iii has bass 3.

## Key terms

- **Bass line**: the lowest note of each chord, heard as a melody.
- **Transcribe**: write down what you hear.
