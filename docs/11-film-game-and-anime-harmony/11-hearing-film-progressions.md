# Hearing film progressions

<span class="tag tag-ear">Ear</span>

After this lesson you can hear the royal road, an Aeolian loop, the minor iv, the Picardy third, the Mario cadence, chromatic mediants and a pedal point, and name each one from a short example.

Before this: [The royal road progression](01-the-royal-road.md), [Aeolian loops](04-aeolian-loops.md), [Borrowed chords and the Mario cadence](05-borrowed-chords-and-the-mario-cadence.md), [The minor iv and the Picardy third](06-minor-iv-and-the-picardy-third.md), [Pedal points and ostinatos](07-pedal-points-and-ostinatos.md), [Chromatic mediants](09-chromatic-mediants.md). You need: your piano and the play bar on this page.

## The idea

### Three questions

Listen to each example three times and ask a different question each time.

1. How does it end? Does the last chord settle at home, or stay unsettled, or turn brighter or darker than you expect?
2. What does the bass do? Does it climb in steps, fall, stay on one note, or loop round?
3. Does one chord sound like it comes from outside the key? That is a borrowed chord or a chromatic mediant.

### What each one sounds like

| Progression | Numerals | Listen for |
|---|---|---|
| Royal road | IV V iii vi | Two chords that climb, then a drop, ending on a minor chord that is not quite home |
| Aeolian loop | i VI III VII | A minor key with three major chords, no strong pull back, and a loop that can keep going |
| Minor iv | I IV iv I | A major chord that darkens by one note, then a smooth fall back home |
| Picardy third | i iv V I | A minor key whose last chord is major, like a door opening |
| Mario cadence | bVI bVII I | Three bass notes climbing in steps into the tonic |
| Chromatic mediant | I bVI I bIII | A jump to a bright chord that shares one note with home |
| Pedal point | I over V | One bass note that does not move while the chords above change and tension builds |

Play each progression on your own piano first, so you know the sound from the inside. The more often you play them, the faster you hear them.

## Try it

Exercise 1. An example starts in a minor key and the last chord is major. Which two things could it be, and how do you tell them apart?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It could be a Picardy third, or a move to the relative or parallel major. A Picardy third changes only the last chord, so the rest of the example stays in the minor key.

</details>

Exercise 2. A loop of four chords repeats without any strong pull to the first chord, and three of the chords sound bright. What is it likely to be?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

An Aeolian loop such as i VI III VII.

</details>

Exercise 3. You hear a bass note that stays the same while three different chords play above it. What is this called?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A pedal point.

</details>

## Common mistakes

Listening only to the top line. Chord types show up in the bass and in the last chord. Sing the bass notes.

Guessing by the key rather than the sound. A progression sounds the same in any key. Do not wait for the key to tell you.

Hearing the Picardy third as a new key. It changes only the last chord. If the whole example brightens, it is a key change, as in the next lesson.

## Key terms

- **Self-test**: a set of examples you identify by ear and then check against the answers.
