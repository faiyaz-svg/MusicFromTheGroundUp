# Transposition

After this lesson you can move a melody or a chord progression to another key, by interval and by scale-degree numbers, and check the result by ear.

You need: your piano and your guitar.

## The idea

### Same tune, new starting note

Play the first phrase of Ode to Joy (Beethoven) in C major, then play it again starting on a different note.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=80
K:C
E E F G | G F E D | C C D E | E3/2 D/ D2 |]
```

Now the same phrase in G major. It sounds higher but it is the same tune.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=80
K:G
B B c d | d c B A | G G A B | B3/2 A/ A2 |]
```

To **transpose** is to write or play music in a different key. Every note moves by the same interval, so the pattern of steps between notes stays the same. Here the interval is a perfect 5th: C up to G is a perfect 5th, which is 7 half steps. E becomes B, F becomes C, D becomes A.

### Transposing by interval

Follow these steps.

1. Name the original key and the new key.
2. Find the interval from the old tonic to the new tonic. Here, C up to G is a perfect 5th.
3. Move every note up (or down) by that interval, keeping the quality.
4. Write the new key signature, and then check every accidental.

Step 3 is where mistakes happen. Moving by letter names alone gives wrong notes in some places. The major scale shows it: C, D, E, F, G, A, B moved up a 5th by letters gives G, A, B, C, D, E, F, and that last F must be F#. The G major scale is G, A, B, C, D, E, F#, G, and the key signature of G major has one sharp. If you write the new key signature first, the F# arrives by itself.

### Transposing with numbers

The scale degrees from the major scale lesson give a faster way. Ode to Joy in any major key is 3 3 4 5, 5 4 3 2, 1 1 2 3, 3 2 2. To play it in a new key, build the major scale on the new tonic and read the numbers off it.

| Key | Scale | Degree 3 | Degree 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| C major | C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C | E | G |
| G major | G, A, B, C, D, E, F#, G | B | D |
| F major | F, G, A, Bb, C, D, E, F | A | C |

A chord progression moves the same way. I, vi, IV, V is four chords in any major key. In C major it is C, Am, F, G. Up a perfect 5th it is G, Em, C, D. Up a perfect 4th (5 half steps) it is F, Dm, Bb, C.

### Why transpose

A singer may find a song too high or too low, so you move it to a key that fits the voice. A piece may be easier to read or finger in another key. On guitar, a capo transposes: with the capo at fret 2, the shapes you know sound 2 half steps higher, so an Am shape sounds as Bm.

## Try it

Exercise 1. Transpose the progression C, Am, F, G up a major 2nd to D major.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

D, Bm, G, A. D major is D, E, F#, G, A, B, C#, D, so vi is B minor, IV is G and V is A.

</details>

Exercise 2. Write the notes 5 4 3 2 1 in F major.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

C, Bb, A, G, F. The fourth note of F major is Bb, because F major has one flat.

</details>

Exercise 3. Which interval moves a melody from G major to D major?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

G up to D is a perfect 5th, 7 half steps.

</details>

## Common mistakes

Moving every note by letter name alone. The 7th degree needs a sharp or flat to keep the major scale pattern, so check it against the new scale.

Keeping the old key signature. Write the new one first.

Mixing up numbers and chords. In C major, 5 is the note G and V is the chord G. Say which you mean.

## Key terms

- **Transpose**: write or play music in another key by moving every note by the same interval.
- **Transposing by interval**: move each note up or down by the interval between the two tonics.
- **Transposing by numbers**: read scale-degree numbers in the new key's scale.
