# Playing by ear

<span class="tag tag-ear">Ear</span>

After this lesson you can work out a melody and its chords from a recording or from memory, write them down as numbers and chord symbols, and play them back.

Before this: [Singing the scale by number](../03-the-major-scale-and-keys/04-singing-the-scale-by-number.md), [First tunes by ear](../03-the-major-scale-and-keys/12-first-tunes-by-ear.md), [Hearing chord progressions](../07-pedal-texture-and-ambient/08-hearing-progressions.md), [Chord symbols and chord charts](../05-minor-keys-and-chord-charts/04-chord-symbols-and-chord-charts.md), [Transposition](01-transposition.md). You need: your piano or your guitar, a pencil, and a way to play a recording, slowly if you can.

## The idea

### The method

Playing by ear is a skill you build by repeating the same steps on many tunes. This method works for a melody and its chords. Follow the steps in order.

1. Listen to the whole recording three times without touching an instrument. Hum along.
2. Find the key. Hum the note that feels like home, usually the last note of the tune. Find it on your instrument. That is the tonic.
3. Find the first note. Try notes of the major scale from the tonic until one matches, then sing its scale degree number.
4. Work out the melody a phrase at a time, two to four bars. Ask of each note: same, a step up, a step down, or a skip. Write it as numbers.
5. Write the rhythm separately. Clap it against the beat and mark long and short notes.
6. Find the bass. Listen to the lowest note on each strong beat. Try it as a root note: I, IV, V or vi.
7. Test the chord. Play the melody note with each candidate chord. The right chord contains the melody note, or sounds comfortable with it.
8. Write it down as numbers and chord symbols, then transpose it into a second key as a test.
9. Play along with the recording and fix what clashes.

If you can slow a recording down without changing its pitch, or loop four bars, use that. Most music and video players and free apps have a speed control and a loop.

### A worked example: Au Clair de la Lune

Au Clair de la Lune is a traditional French tune. We will work out its first four bars, as if we had heard it. Play it and listen to the shape.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=80
K:C
"C"C C C D | "C"E2 "G"D2 | "C"C E "G"D D | "C"C4 |]
```

Step 2: the tune ends on C, so the key is C major. Step 3: it starts on C, which is degree 1. Step 4: the melody in numbers is 1 1 1 2 | 3 2 | 1 3 2 2 | 1. It moves by step and has one skip (1 to 3 in bar 3). Step 5: the rhythm is steady quarter notes, with two half notes (the 3 and 2 in bar 2) and a long note at the end.

Step 6: the bass is C at the start. Step 7: the D in bar 2 needs a chord that has D in it. C does not (C, E, G), but G does (G, B, D), so G sounds right under D. The same happens in bar 3, where C and E sit over C and the two D notes sit over G. The chord chart is C | C G | C G | C. Other chords may fit too: there is often more than one right answer, and your ear decides.

Step 8: transposed to G major, the melody is G G G A | B A | G B A A | G, and the chords are G | G D | G D | G.

### Practising on music you like

You can use any music you like for private study, including film, game and anime music. Pick a short piece you know well, such as a theme, and work out its melody and chords with the steps above. Write your notes as numbers and chord symbols for yourself, and keep them to yourself. This book does not print the melodies of those pieces, and your notes are for your own practice, not for sharing.

Start with easy ones. A good first target is a tune with a slow melody and two or three chords. Use the lessons on [hearing chord progressions](../09-harmony/11-hearing-progressions-2.md) and [hearing film progressions](../11-film-game-and-anime-harmony/11-hearing-film-progressions.md) to tune your ear for the chord moves they use.

## Try it

Exercise 1. A tune starts on E and ends on C. What is the key, and what degree is the first note?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

C major. E is degree 3.

</details>

Exercise 2. The melody note is E. Which of C, G and F could be under it?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

C (C, E, G) has E in it. G (G, B, D) and F (F, A, C) do not.

</details>

Exercise 3. Write the numbers 1 1 5 5 6 6 5 in the key of F.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

F F C C D D C. F major is F, G, A, Bb, C, D, E, F, so degree 5 is C and degree 6 is D.

</details>

### Practice routine (30 minutes)

| Minutes | What to do | Tempo | Listen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up: sing the major scale by number over a drone, then play it | 60 bpm | Singing the same pitch as the key |
| 15 | Work out one new tune with the nine steps: melody first, then bass and chords | Untimed | The tonic, then the chord changes |
| 5 | Play the finished tune in a second key | 60 bpm | The same tune in a new key |
| 5 | Guitar: play the chords you found, strumming one per bar | 60 bpm | Clean changes |

Use a new tune each time you repeat the routine, for at least three days.

- [ ] You can work out a tune of eight bars from a recording and play it from memory in one key.
- [ ] You can find a chord chart for the same tune with I, IV and V (and vi if needed).
- [ ] You can play it in a second key without looking.
- [ ] You can sing the numbers of a tune before you play it.

## Common mistakes

Starting on the first note without finding the key. Find the tonic first.

Working out the melody and the rhythm at the same time. Do the pitches, then the rhythm.

Trusting the first chord that fits. Try the other chords and listen.

## Key terms

- **Playing by ear**: working out music by listening, without a score.
- **Tonic**: the home note of the key.
- **Root**: the note a chord is named after.
