# Play along with yourself

<span class="tag tag-other">Practice</span>

After this lesson you can record one part of a piece on your phone or your piano, and play a second part over the recording, in time.

Before this: [Chords from a lead sheet on piano](../05-minor-keys-and-chord-charts/06-chords-from-a-lead-sheet-on-piano.md), [Chord symbols and chord charts](../05-minor-keys-and-chord-charts/04-chord-symbols-and-chord-charts.md), [One-minute chord changes](../02-two-hands-and-first-chords/11-one-minute-changes.md), [Hearing chord progressions](../07-pedal-texture-and-ambient/08-hearing-progressions.md). You need: your piano, your guitar, a phone (or a piano with a recorder) and a metronome.

## The idea

### Why play with a recording of yourself

You own one piano and one guitar, and you play one at a time. A recording of one part lets you play the other part against it. It also trains you to keep time, to listen to another line, and to fit in. Fitting in is what happens when you play with other people.

### Recording one part

The phone is the simplest tool. Open its voice recorder or camera app and do this.

1. Choose a short loop of four bars, such as the one below, and decide the tempo. Use 70 bpm.
2. Put the phone about 30 centimetres from the soundhole of the guitar, or near the piano's speakers. If you play the digital piano with headphones, record with the piano's own recorder instead if it has one, or plug your phone's cable into the piano's output (check the piano's manual).
3. Start the metronome and the recording. Count four clicks in your head, then play the loop at least four times through without stopping.
4. Stop the recording, play it back, and check that the tempo is steady and that the part is even.

Some digital pianos have a built-in recorder. If yours does, check its manual: it records what you play, and plays it back while you play over it. Use whichever method records cleanly.

### Part one: the chords

This is a four-bar loop of chords in C major, written for this lesson: C, Am, F and G, one per bar. Play it as whole-note triads on the piano.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=70
K:C
%%score {RH LH}
V:RH clef=treble
V:LH clef=bass
V:RH
"C"[CEG]4 | "Am"[CEA]4 | "F"[CFA]4 | "G"[B,DG]4 |]
V:LH
C,4 | A,,4 | F,,4 | G,,4 |]
```

### Part two: the melody

This is a melody written for this lesson, to play over the loop. The first example is for guitar, written an octave above where it sounds.

```abc
%%opts tab=guitar
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=70
K:C clef=treble-8
E G c G | A c e c | c A F A | G B d2 |]
```

The melody notes of each bar come from the chord of that bar: C, E and G in bar 1, A, C and E in bar 2, F, A and C in bar 3, G, B and D in bar 4. That is why they fit.

### Playing the other part

1. Play the recording and listen once without playing.
2. Count in with the recording, then play the melody over it.
3. Aim for the first note of each bar to land when the chord changes.
4. Record both parts together with your phone if you want to hear them as a whole.

Then swap. Record the guitar playing C, Am, F and G, one strum per bar or four. Play the piano melody over it with your right hand. The melody for the piano is the same notes, an octave higher than the guitar example.

Over several days, build a playlist of loops: a chord loop, a bass line, a rhythm. Re-record when you improve.

Exercise 3. Your melody notes land a little before the chord changes on the recording. What do you do?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Play bar by bar with the metronome and wait for the downbeat. Record the part again if your own recording drifts.

</details>

<div class="practice" markdown>

### Practice routine (30 minutes)

| Minutes | What to do | Tempo | Listen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up: C, Am, F and G chords on guitar, one per bar | 70 bpm | Clean changes |
| 10 | Record the four-bar chord loop on piano, four times through, then check it | 70 bpm | A steady tempo |
| 10 | Play the guitar melody over the recording, then record the result | 70 bpm | Landing on beat 1 with the chord change |
| 5 | Piano: play the melody with your right hand over a guitar chord recording from another day | 70 bpm | Fitting in with the other part |

Repeat on at least three days, with a new loop or a new melody each time.

</div>

### Move on when

- [ ] You can record a four-bar loop with a steady tempo, four times through.
- [ ] You can play the melody over your recording, three times in a row, with each chord change landing on beat 1.
- [ ] You can do the same with the parts swapped.
- [ ] You can name what you would fix after listening to the two parts together.

## Common mistakes

Recording without a count-in. The loop then starts off the beat, and everything you play over it feels late.

Recording at a tempo you can barely play. Choose a tempo where the part is easy.

Playing along without listening. Aim to hear the other part while you play.

## Key terms

- **Loop**: a short section that repeats.
- **Count-in**: clicks or counting before a recording starts.
- **Play-along**: playing a part over a recording.
