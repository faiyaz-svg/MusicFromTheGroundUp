## The idea

### RCM exams

The Royal Conservatory of Music (RCM) runs graded music exams in Canada. They have levels for practical playing (piano and guitar among them) and for theory. Many Ontario teachers prepare students for them, and the levels give a shared way to describe how far a student has gone. This book's target is roughly RCM Level 5 to 6 piano and RCM Theory 6 to 7, as it says on the first page of the book.

Exams are optional. They give you a deadline, a repertoire list, a checklist of scales and ear tests, and a certificate. If you like having those, they help. If not, you can use the syllabus as a reading list without sitting an exam. The syllabi, exam dates and fees change, so read the RCM's own pages for the current ones (as of 2026): [rcmusic.com/learning/examinations](https://www.rcmusic.com/learning/examinations). Check the repertoire lists on that site before you choose pieces, and don't assume that a piece in this book is on a list.

### Finding a teacher

A teacher sees what a book cannot: your hand shape, your tension, your timing. A few lessons can fix a habit that would take months to find alone. In Ontario you can ask local music schools and community music schools, ask a music store, or ask a friend who plays. Take a trial lesson, and ask three questions.

1. Do you teach adults?
2. Which instrument, and which exams, do you prepare students for?
3. Can I bring a piece from this book, such as the Bach bourrée or a Chopin prelude, to work on?

Online lessons are another choice. Pick the teacher by the same questions.

### Keeping a routine after the book

The 30-minute routine still works. Keep its shape and change the content.

| Minutes | What to do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up: a scale and an arpeggio, a different key each week |
| 15 | The piece you are learning, one section at a time |
| 5 | Sight reading: a new, easy piece every day |
| 5 | Ear or improvising: work out a tune, or improvise over a loop |

Once a week, play an old piece all the way through. Once a month, record yourself, listen the next day, and write down one thing to fix. Keep a list of pieces you can play.

### Working through your own sheet music

You can use the method from this book's piece lessons on any score. Choose a piece that looks about one level below your best, and follow these steps.

1. Find the key and the time signature. Name the scale and the chords.
2. Find the form. Mark the sections by bar number and label them A, B and so on.
3. Find the hard spots. Mark every bar with an accidental, a leap, a change of position or a fast run.
4. Split it into sections of two to four bars, and write the order you will learn them in.
5. Work hands separately, slowly, with a metronome, until you can play each section three times in a row without a stop.
6. Join the sections two at a time. Add the pedal or the picking pattern last.
7. Keep the plan in weeks, and record the piece at the end.

If you do not know a symbol, look it up in the glossary or ask a teacher.

### Practice routine (30 minutes)

| Minutes | What to do | Tempo | Listen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up: a major scale and its arpeggio in a key you have not played this week | 72 bpm | Even notes |
| 15 | Start the plan for your chosen piece: the first section, hands separately | 50 bpm | Three clean in a row |
| 5 | Sight reading: a new easy piece | 60 bpm | Keep going after a wrong note |
| 5 | Ear: work out the first phrase of a tune you know | Untimed | The tonic |

Repeat on at least three days, then continue with the next section of your plan.

## Common mistakes

Choosing a piece that is far too hard. A piece one level below your best teaches more than one far above.

Stopping the routine when the book ends. Keep the 30 minutes and change the content.

Skipping the plan. Without bar numbers and sections, long pieces drift.

## Key terms

- **RCM**: the Royal Conservatory of Music, which sets graded exams in Canada.
- **Repertoire**: the pieces you can play.
- **Licensed arrangement**: sheet music published with the permission of the owner of the music.
